Alan Turing's learning machine (1950)

In §7 of Computing Machinery and Intelligence Turing argues that programming an adult mind by hand is hopeless. Instead, build a child machine — little mechanism, many “blank sheets” — and educate it.

Random element in the machineStochastic gradient descent, dropout
Hereditary material · mutation · selectionArchitecture search, evolutionary methods
Teacher ignorant of inner workingsThe interpretability problem
The theory in one loop — experience changes the machine:
$$\underbrace{\theta_{t+1}}_{\text{new rules}} \;=\; \theta_t \;+\; \eta\; \underbrace{\mathcal{F}\big(\text{reward}_t,\ \text{error}_t\big)}_{\text{teacher's signal}}$$
Same template as the perceptron rule (1958), back-prop (1986) and policy-gradient RL.

Unorganised machines (1948)

Turing's NPL report described networks of randomly connected two-input “neurons” whose connections can be switched on or off by training — a neural network a decade before the perceptron.

Turing also predicted the evaluation problem: the imitation game judges behaviour, not mechanism — still how we benchmark LLMs.
Key message: the core of modern ML — learn by adjusting internal parameters from feedback — is already in Turing 1950. Turing even anticipated that the teacher would not know exactly what happens inside the learner: today's interpretability problem. Mention his 1948 report on unorganised machines (published posthumously, 1968) as an early neural-network concept.

Rules first, then two winters

1956–1974

Symbolic optimism

Logic, search and hand-written rules (Logic Theorist, GPS). Perceptrons learned simple patterns. Claims ran far ahead of results.

1974–1980 · winter I

Reality check

Combinatorial explosion, tiny computers, the XOR critique of single-layer perceptrons and the UK Lighthill report cut funding.

1980–1987

Expert systems

Knowledge engineered as IF–THEN rules (MYCIN, XCON). Worked in narrow domains, but brittle and costly to maintain.

1987–1993 · winter II

Lisp-machine market collapses; rule bases do not scale. Meanwhile back-prop (1986) quietly revives neural networks.

Lesson for EO

Decision-tree land-cover rules and spectral thresholds (e.g. NDWI > 0 = water) are symbolic AI. They work locally and break across sensors, seasons and regions.

Make the link to EO practice: many operational products still rely on thresholds and rule-based decision trees. They are transparent, but brittle when the domain shifts — exactly the failure mode that pushed AI toward learning from data.

The paradigm

Hand-designed features → a shallow model with a clean objective (often convex) and guarantees from statistical learning theory: control the capacity, not just the training error.

In remote sensing

Gaussian maximum-likelihood classifier (1970s–90s) → SVM for hyperspectral data (2000s) → random forest as the default in Earth Engine (2010s).

Still alive

On tabular features, tuned tree ensembles still match or beat deep nets (Grinsztajn et al., 2022). Cheap, strong baselines for every EO project.

Naive Bayes — classify by probability

click adds:
$$P(k\mid\mathbf{x})=\frac{P(k)\,p(\mathbf{x}\mid k)}{\sum_{k'}P(k')\,p(\mathbf{x}\mid k')}\;\overset{\text{naive}}{\propto}\;P(k)\prod_{j=1}^{d}p(x_j\mid k)$$ $$p(x_j\mid k)=\mathcal{N}\big(x_j;\,\mu_{kj},\sigma^2_{kj}\big),\qquad \hat y=\arg\max_k\Big[\log P(k)+\sum_j\log p(x_j\mid k)\Big]$$

“Naive” = bands independent given the class

Each class is a product of 1-D Gaussians, so its ellipses stay aligned with the axes. Training = means and variances per class: one pass, works with a handful of pixels.

EO classic · maximum-likelihood classifier

Keep the full covariance: \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid k)\propto|\Sigma_k|^{-1/2}e^{-\frac12(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top\Sigma_k^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu_k)}\). Ellipses tilt along the soil line; boundaries become quadratic. The standard supervised classifier of remote sensing for decades.

Demo: start in naive mode — the soil / built-up cloud is elongated along the soil line, but the naive ellipses cannot tilt, so they spread probability over empty space and bulge towards the vegetation cloud. Switch to full Σ: the ellipses rotate onto the soil line and the boundary bends (dark rings mark misclassified pixels). Add a few soil pixels near the vegetation tip to see the two models disagree. Hover anywhere to read the three posteriors; click to add training pixels of the selected class and watch the class prior and the boundary move. Note that correlated bands violate the naive assumption, which makes the probabilities over-confident even when the argmax is right (Domingos & Pazzani 1997).

Decision trees — learned IF–THEN rules

$$\text{Gini}(S)=1-\sum_k p_k^2\qquad H(S)=-\sum_k p_k\log_2 p_k$$ $$\Delta I(j,t)=I(S)-\tfrac{|S_L|}{|S|}\,I(S_L)-\tfrac{|S_R|}{|S|}\,I(S_R)$$
Greedy: at every node try each band \(j\) and threshold \(t\) (split \(x_j\le t\)); keep the largest impurity drop \(\Delta I\); recurse.

The learned rules (top two levels)


      
Deeper ⇒ training accuracy → 100 %, test accuracy falls: high variance. A new sample gives a different tree — the problem ensembles solve.

Bagging & random forests — many noisy trees, one calm vote



  

    

      
      

        
        
        
      

      

    

    

      

        $$\mathbb{E}\big[(y-\hat f)^2\big]=\text{Bias}^2+\text{Variance}+\sigma^2_\varepsilon$$
        $$\hat f_{\text{bag}}(\mathbf{x})=\operatorname{vote}\{T_b(\mathbf{x})\}_{b=1}^{B},\qquad \operatorname{Var}\big[\bar T\big]=\rho\,\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2$$
      

      
Two sources of randomness
    
        
  • Bootstrap: each tree sees \(n\) pixels drawn with replacement; averaging \(B\) trees shrinks the \(\tfrac{1-\rho}{B}\) term.
    • 
        
  • Random features: each split tries only \(m\approx\sqrt{p}\) bands, so trees decorrelate and \(\rho\) drops.
    • 
        
  • Out-of-bag: \((1-\tfrac1n)^n\to e^{-1}\approx\) 37 % of pixels are unused by each tree — a free validation set.

      
EO workhorse (ee.Classifier.smileRandomForest): robust to label noise and to 100+ stacked features. Prefer permutation importance — impurity importance is biased.

    

  




Demo: “1 deep tree” shows the jagged, over-fitted map from the previous slide. Switch to Bagging and slide B from 1 to 100: the islands melt away, the colour intensity (vote share) shows where the forest is unsure, and test accuracy rises. Random forest adds feature subsampling (here one random band per split, because we only have two). OOB accuracy tracks test accuracy without any held-out data. Bias is unchanged — that is what boosting attacks next.







Boosting I · AdaBoost — learn from your mistakes



  

    

      
      

        
        
        
        
        
      

      

    

    

      

        $$\varepsilon_t=\sum_i w_i\,\mathbb{1}\big[h_t(\mathbf{x}_i)\neq y_i\big],\qquad \alpha_t=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}$$
        $$w_i\leftarrow\frac{w_i\,e^{-\alpha_t\,y_i\,h_t(\mathbf{x}_i)}}{Z_t},\qquad H(\mathbf{x})=\operatorname{sign}\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_t\,h_t(\mathbf{x})\Big)$$
      

      
Sequential, not parallel
Each weak learner — a one-split stump (dashed gold line) — trains on re-weighted pixels: the ones the ensemble still gets wrong grow bigger. AdaBoost is stage-wise minimisation of the exponential loss \(e^{-yF(\mathbf{x})}\).

      
Try it
Seasons (XOR): single-season crops vs bare land and orchards in (NDVI March, NDVI July). A sum of stumps uses one band at a time and cannot model the interaction — switch to depth-2 trees. Bagging cuts variance; boosting cuts bias, but it chases mislabelled pixels.

    

  




Demo: on the ring, press +1 a few times. Each stump is a single threshold; the misclassified pixels swell; α is large when ε is small. Press +25: the staircase boundary wraps the ring. Then Seasons (XOR) with stumps — training error stays high because an additive model of single-band rules cannot express “high in March XOR high in July”. Switch to depth-2 trees and it is solved in a few rounds. In practice, boosting on noisy reference polygons can over-fit the label noise; gradient boosting with shrinkage and early stopping is the modern form.







Boosting II · Gradient boosting — fit the residuals, step by step



  

    

      
      

        
        
        
        
      

      

    

    

      

        $$r_i^{(m)}=-\left[\frac{\partial\,\ell\big(y_i,F(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)}{\partial F(\mathbf{x}_i)}\right]_{F=F_{m-1}}$$
        $$h_m=\arg\min_h\sum_i\big(r_i^{(m)}-h(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)^2,\qquad F_m=F_{m-1}+\nu\,h_m$$
        
Squared loss ⇒ \(r_i=y_i-F(\mathbf{x}_i)\): literally fit the residuals. Absolute loss ⇒ \(r_i=\operatorname{sign}(y_i-F)\): cloudy dips stop pulling the curve down.

      

      
XGBoost · LightGBM · CatBoost
Newton (second-order) steps and regularised leaves, \(\mathcal{L}=\sum_i\ell(y_i,\hat y_i)+\sum_k\big(\gamma T_k+\tfrac12\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}_k\rVert^2\big)\); histogram splits, missing-value handling, GPU training.

      
The go-to model for tabular EO regression: yield, ET, soil moisture, biomass from bands, weather and terrain features.

    

  




Demo: each click fits a small tree (gold, bottom panel) to the current residuals (blue bars) and adds ν times it to the model (terracotta). With ν = 1 it fits fast but chases noise; ν = 0.1–0.3 needs more trees and generalises better (shrinkage). Now look at the cloud dips: with squared loss the curve sags towards them; switch to absolute loss — the gradient is only the sign of the residual, so outliers have bounded influence. The loss you choose is the gradient you fit: that is the whole idea of gradient boosting, and of deep learning too.







Support vector machines — the widest street



  

    

      
      

        
        
        
        
        
      

      

    

    

      

        $$\min_{\mathbf{w},b,\boldsymbol\xi}\ \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2+C\sum_i\xi_i\quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i+b)\ge1-\xi_i,\ \ \xi_i\ge0$$
        
margin width \(=2/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\) · same as hinge loss \(\sum_i\max\big(0,1-y_if(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)+\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2\)

        $$f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i\in\text{SV}}\alpha_i\,y_i\,K(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x})+b,\qquad K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')=e^{-\gamma\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rVert^2}$$
      

      
Kernel trick & sparsity
Only the support vectors (gold rings) define the boundary. A kernel computes dot products in a high-dimensional feature space without building it. Small \(C\): wide, tolerant margin; large \(C\) and \(\gamma\): tight, wiggly fit.

      
SVMs ruled hyperspectral classification in the 2000s: hundreds of bands, few labelled pixels — where the margin principle shines. Scale features first.

    

  




Demo: linear kernel on water vs vegetation — solid line f = 0, dashed lines f = ±1, the light band between them is the margin; only the ringed points matter (delete any other point and nothing changes). Lower C: the margin widens and more points become support vectors. Switch to Seasons (XOR) — no line works; RBF kernel solves it. Push γ to the maximum and C high: islands around single pixels = over-fitting. Click to add vegetation pixels, shift-click (or right-click) for the other class. The optimiser running in the browser is Platt's simplified SMO.

The task
Only \(\{\mathbf{x}_i\}\), no \(y\). Find structure in \(p(\mathbf{x})\): groups (clustering), directions (dimensionality reduction), densities (mixtures, anomalies).

    
In remote sensing
Unsupervised classification (k-means / ISODATA, then an analyst names the clusters), PCA / MNF for multi- and hyperspectral data, PCA-based change detection.

    
Why it matters now
Labels are scarce, pixels are not. Self-supervised pre-training (Part VIII) is the modern heir: learn the structure of unlabelled archives first.

  




Unsupervised learning answers “what is in my data?” before anyone has labelled it. In EO it was the first practical form of machine learning: ISODATA clustering in the image-processing packages of the 1970s–90s, PCA for multispectral compression and change detection. Five demos follow: k-means step by step, k-means as an unsupervised land-cover map, Gaussian mixtures with EM, DBSCAN, and PCA.







k-means — assign, update, repeat



  

    

      
      

        
        
        
      

      

    

    

      

        $$J=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\lVert\mathbf{x}_i-\boldsymbol\mu_{c(i)}\big\rVert^2$$
        $$\text{assign: } c(i)=\arg\min_k\lVert\mathbf{x}_i-\boldsymbol\mu_k\rVert^2\qquad \text{update: } \boldsymbol\mu_k=\frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{i\in C_k}\mathbf{x}_i$$
      

      
Coordinate descent on J
Each step can only lower \(J\), so the algorithm always converges — but to a local minimum that depends on the start. k-means++ picks each new centre with probability \(\propto D(\mathbf{x})^2\), the squared distance to the nearest centre already chosen.

      
k-means assumes round, equal-size clusters and you must choose \(k\). Try k = 2 and k = 7, or several random inits.

    

  




Demo: alternate Assign (pixels join the nearest centroid; Voronoi cells shaded) and Update (centroids jump to the mean of their pixels). Run iterates until no pixel changes. New init with random starts sometimes ends with two centroids sharing one group — a local minimum; k-means++ avoids most of those. The four groups are water, dense vegetation, sparse vegetation and soil in (Red, NIR) space.

The classic workflow
  1. stack bands, standardise
  2. cluster all pixels (k-means / ISODATA)
  3. an analyst labels each cluster
  4. merge or split clusters, check accuracy

      
Try it
Visible bands only: fields and roofs blur together. Add NIR and SWIR: purity against the true cover jumps — more spectral information, better clusters.

    

  




This is how many first land-cover maps were made: no training data, just clustering 25 600 pixels into k spectral groups, then naming them. The purity number compares each cluster with the true dominant cover of its pixels (which only we know, because the scene is synthetic). ISODATA adds rules to split clusters that are too spread out and merge those that are too close, so k adapts.







Gaussian mixtures — soft clusters, learned by EM



  

    

      
      

        
        
      

      

    

    

      

        $$p(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k)$$
        $$\textbf{E: }\ \gamma_{ik}=\frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_j\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}\qquad \textbf{M: }\ N_k=\sum_i\gamma_{ik},\ \ \boldsymbol\mu_k=\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}\mathbf{x}_i$$
        
\(\Sigma_k=\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i\gamma_{ik}(\mathbf{x}_i-\boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf{x}_i-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top,\ \ \pi_k=N_k/n\)

      

      
EM in one sentence
Guess who belongs where (E), refit each Gaussian to its weighted pixels (M), repeat. The log-likelihood never decreases. k-means is the hard-assignment limit of EM with equal round covariances.

      
Soft memberships suit mixed pixels: a 10 m pixel can be 60 % crop and 40 % soil. The tilted ellipses are the supervised MLC's Gaussians — learned without labels.

    

  




Demo: start from a fresh init and alternate E and M. Point colours blend the cluster colours by their responsibilities γ, so uncertain pixels between clusters look mixed. Ellipses grow and tilt to follow the elongated vegetation and soil-line clouds, which k-means cannot do. Watch the log-likelihood climb and flatten.







DBSCAN — clusters of any shape, plus noise



  

    

      
      

        
        
        
      

      

    

    

      

        $$N_\varepsilon(\mathbf{p})=\{\mathbf{q}:\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\rVert\le\varepsilon\},\qquad \mathbf{p}\ \text{is core}\iff|N_\varepsilon(\mathbf{p})|\ge\text{minPts}$$
        
A cluster = all points density-reachable from a core point · border points are reached but not core · the rest is noise

      

      
No k, no round shapes
Clusters grow through chains of dense neighbourhoods, so they follow rivers and roads. Isolated points stay noise (grey ×). Hover to see the ε-disc around the cursor.

      
EO uses: grouping building or ship detections, clustering fire hotspots into fire events, cleaning GNSS or LiDAR point clouds.

    

  




The points could be building detections from a model, or VIIRS fire hotspots: a settlement strung along a river, a second curved one, a compact village, and scattered false alarms. Switch to k-means (k = 3): it cuts the curved groups into round pieces and must assign every false alarm to some cluster. With DBSCAN, make ε too small and everything becomes noise; too large and the settlements merge. minPts sets how dense “dense” is.







PCA — the directions of greatest variance



  

    

      
      

        
        
      

      

    

    

      

        $$\Sigma=\tfrac1n\sum_i(\mathbf{x}_i-\bar{\mathbf{x}})(\mathbf{x}_i-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^\top,\qquad \Sigma\,\mathbf{v}_k=\lambda_k\mathbf{v}_k,\qquad z_{ik}=\mathbf{v}_k^\top(\mathbf{x}_i-\bar{\mathbf{x}})$$
        
\(\mathbf{v}_1=\arg\max_{\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert=1}\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{x})\) · explained variance of PC \(k\) = \(\lambda_k/\sum_j\lambda_j\)

What is different
No labels — only a scalar reward, often delayed. The agent's own actions choose its data, so it must balance exploring against exploiting.

    
In Earth observation
Satellite tasking and scheduling, UAV survey paths, adaptive field sampling, irrigation and reservoir control — and RLHF behind the LLM agents of Part X.

  




Turing already proposed rewards and punishments (Part I); Bellman gave the mathematics (dynamic programming), Sutton and Watkins the learning algorithms (TD, Q-learning). Deep networks then turned tables into function approximators: DQN, AlphaGo, and today RL from human feedback, which aligns the LLMs we use for agents. The demos that follow use one toy EO problem: a survey drone that must reach a flooded village while avoiding storm cells.







Markov decision processes — the Bellman equation



  

    

      
      

        
        
        
      

      

    

    

      

        MDP \((\mathcal S,\mathcal A,P,R,\gamma)\) · return \(G_t=\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^k r_{t+k+1}\) · policy \(\pi(a\mid s)\)
        $$V^*(s)=\max_a\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\big[R(s,a,s')+\gamma\,V^*(s')\big]$$
        $$V_{k+1}(s)\leftarrow\max_a\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\big[R+\gamma V_k(s')\big],\qquad \pi^*(s)=\arg\max_a Q^*(s,a)$$
      

      
Planning with a known model
Value iteration applies the Bellman update to every state. It is a \(\gamma\)-contraction, so \(V_k\to V^*\) from any start; values spread outward from the rewards, sweep by sweep.

      
Try it
Raise slip (wind pushes the drone sideways): the policy leaves the short path between the storms for a safer detour. Lower γ: the far village stops being worth it. Click a free cell to add a mountain, then a storm.

    

  




The drone gets −0.1 per move (battery), +10 at the flooded village, −10 if it enters a storm cell; mountains block. With slip > 0 an action goes sideways with that probability. The slide opens with converged values; press “Reset V”, then “1 sweep” a few times: values propagate one step per sweep from the goal, arrows show the greedy policy. This needs the transition model P — the next slide removes that assumption.







Q-learning — learn values from experience



  

    

      
      
      

        
        
        
      

      

    

    

      

        $$Q(s,a)\leftarrow Q(s,a)+\alpha\Big[\underbrace{r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')}_{\text{TD target}}-Q(s,a)\Big]$$
        
ε-greedy: random action with probability ε, otherwise \(\arg\max_a Q(s,a)\) · SARSA uses the action actually taken instead of the max

      

      
Model-free
No transition model: the drone only sees \((s,a,r,s')\) and bootstraps from its own estimates (temporal-difference learning). Q-learning is off-policy: it learns the greedy policy while behaving ε-greedily.

      
Exploration matters
With ε = 0 the drone may lock onto the first route that worked. Too much ε and it keeps flying into storms. Learning rate α trades speed for noise.

Multi-armed bandits — explore or exploit?



  

    

      
      
      

        
        
        
        
        
        
      

      

    

    

      

        Regret after \(T\) steps: \(\;R_T=T\mu^*-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu_{a_t}\)
        $$\text{UCB1: }a_t=\arg\max_a\Big[\hat\mu_a+c\sqrt{\tfrac{\ln t}{N_a}}\Big]$$
        $$\text{Thompson: }\tilde\mu_a\sim\text{Beta}(1+S_a,\,1+N_a-S_a),\ \ a_t=\arg\max_a\tilde\mu_a$$
      

      
RL with one state
Five field sites, each with an unknown chance of detecting an event per visit. Every visit spent learning about a poor site is a lost detection. ε-greedy explores blindly; UCB is optimistic about uncertain sites; Thompson samples from its beliefs.

      
Bandits are used for adaptive sampling, sensor tasking and A/B testing — and their logic (optimism, posterior sampling) runs inside full RL agents.

From tables to networks: deep RL and RLHF



  

    

      
DQN — a CNN replaces the Q-table; replay buffer + target network \(\theta^-\):
        $$\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q_{\theta^-}(s',a')-Q_\theta(s,a)\big)^2\Big]$$

      
Policy gradient (REINFORCE) — learn \(\pi_\theta\) directly:
        $$\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\Big[\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,G_t\Big]$$

      
PPO — stable updates with a clipped ratio \(\rho_t=\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)/\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t|s_t)\):
        $$L^{\text{CLIP}}=\mathbb{E}_t\big[\min\big(\rho_t\hat A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t\big)\big]$$

Tabular methods do not scale to images or continuous states, so networks (Parts III–VII) approximate Q or π. DQN learned Atari from pixels; AlphaGo combined policy and value networks with tree search; PPO is the workhorse optimiser. RLHF reuses PPO to fine-tune language models on a learned reward of human preferences, with a KL penalty that keeps the model close to its supervised starting point — this is the alignment step behind the LLMs in Part X. In EO, RL is still mostly research: rewards are hard to define and simulators are needed to train safely.

Deep learning is matrix multiplication — chips made it cheap



  

    

      

        
ChipDesignGood at

        
CPUFew powerful cores, big cachesBranchy, sequential code

        
GPUThousands of simple cores, SIMTParallel multiply–add on tensors

        
Tensor coreMatrix-multiply unit inside a GPUMixed precision (FP16/BF16/FP8)

        
TPUSystolic array (Google, 2015–16)Dense matmul at scale

      

      
One dense layer = one matmul: \(\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\). For a batch of 256 Sentinel-2 patches through ResNet-50 that is ≈ 1–2 TFLOP per forward pass — seconds on a CPU, milliseconds on a GPU.

      
2012: AlexNet trained on two consumer GTX 580 GPUs and won ImageNet by a wide margin — the moment the field switched.

    

    
  




The ResNet-50 number: about 4 G multiply–adds (≈ 8 GFLOP) per 224×224 image, so a batch of 256 is on the order of 1–2 TFLOP. Emphasise orders of magnitude, not exact marketing numbers.
