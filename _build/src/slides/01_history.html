Alan Turing's learning machine (1950)

In §7 of Computing Machinery and Intelligence Turing argues that programming an adult mind by hand is hopeless. Instead, build a child machine — little mechanism, many “blank sheets” — and educate it.

Turing's ideaModern counterpart
Child machine + educationModel with random init + training on data
Rewards & punishmentsReinforcement learning, RLHF for LLMs
Changing “rules” during learningUpdating parameters θ
Random element in the machineStochastic gradient descent, dropout
Hereditary material · mutation · selectionArchitecture search, evolutionary methods
Teacher ignorant of inner workingsThe interpretability problem
The theory in one loop — experience changes the machine:
$$\underbrace{\theta_{t+1}}_{\text{new rules}} \;=\; \theta_t \;+\; \eta\; \underbrace{\mathcal{F}\big(\text{reward}_t,\ \text{error}_t\big)}_{\text{teacher's signal}}$$
Same template as the perceptron rule (1958), back-prop (1986) and policy-gradient RL.

Unorganised machines (1948)

Turing's NPL report described networks of randomly connected two-input “neurons” whose connections can be switched on or off by training — a neural network a decade before the perceptron.

Turing also predicted the evaluation problem: the imitation game judges behaviour, not mechanism — still how we benchmark LLMs.
Key message: the core of modern ML — learn by adjusting internal parameters from feedback — is already in Turing 1950. Turing even anticipated that the teacher would not know exactly what happens inside the learner: today's interpretability problem. Mention his 1948 report on unorganised machines (published posthumously, 1968) as an early neural-network concept.

Rules first, then two winters

1956–1974

Symbolic optimism

Logic, search and hand-written rules (Logic Theorist, GPS). Perceptrons learned simple patterns. Claims ran far ahead of results.

1974–1980 · winter I

Reality check

Combinatorial explosion, tiny computers, the XOR critique of single-layer perceptrons and the UK Lighthill report cut funding.

1980–1987

Expert systems

Knowledge engineered as IF–THEN rules (MYCIN, XCON). Worked in narrow domains, but brittle and costly to maintain.

1987–1993 · winter II

Lisp-machine market collapses; rule bases do not scale. Meanwhile back-prop (1986) quietly revives neural networks.

Lesson for EO

Decision-tree land-cover rules and spectral thresholds (e.g. NDWI > 0 = water) are symbolic AI. They work locally and break across sensors, seasons and regions.

Make the link to EO practice: many operational products still rely on thresholds and rule-based decision trees. They are transparent, but brittle when the domain shifts — exactly the failure mode that pushed AI toward learning from data.

The classical-ML era: you design the features

Raw bandsB2…B12
→
Hand featuresNDVI, NDWI, texture, stats
→
ModelSVM · RF · XGBoost
  • SVM — maximise the margin; kernels give non-linear boundaries.
  • Random forest — bagged decision trees; robust, fast, gives feature importance. Still the workhorse of land-cover mapping in Earth Engine.
  • Gradient boosting — trees fitted to residuals; strong on tabular EO features.
Limit: performance is capped by the features you thought of. Spatial context, shapes and temporal dynamics are hard to hand-craft.
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import GroupKFold, cross_val_score

# X_bands: (n_pixels, 10) Sentinel-2 L2A reflectances
# y: land-cover label, groups: id of the polygon each pixel came from
def indices(X):
    B2, B3, B4, B8, B11 = X[:, 0], X[:, 1], X[:, 2], X[:, 6], X[:, 8]
    ndvi = (B8 - B4) / (B8 + B4 + 1e-6)
    ndwi = (B3 - B8) / (B3 + B8 + 1e-6)
    ndbi = (B11 - B8) / (B11 + B8 + 1e-6)   # built-up
    return np.c_[X, ndvi, ndwi, ndbi]

F = indices(X_bands)
rf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, n_jobs=-1,
                            class_weight="balanced")
# group-wise CV: pixels of one polygon never in train AND test
scores = cross_val_score(rf, F, y, groups=groups,
                         cv=GroupKFold(5), scoring="f1_macro")
print(f"macro-F1 = {scores.mean():.3f} ± {scores.std():.3f}")
Show the code briefly. Two lessons carry through the whole course: (1) engineered indices are a form of prior knowledge; (2) evaluate with spatially grouped folds, otherwise neighbouring pixels leak between train and test and accuracy is inflated.

Why now? Data × algorithms × compute

◈

Data

  • ImageNet (2009): 14 M labelled images
  • Landsat archive opened free in 2008
  • Copernicus Sentinels: free & open, petabytes per year
  • Labels from crowdsourcing & OpenStreetMap
∂

Algorithms

  • ReLU: gradients that do not vanish
  • Dropout, batch-norm, Adam
  • Residual connections (ResNet): 100+ layers
  • Self-supervised pre-training
▦

Compute

  • GPUs: thousands of cores for matrix maths
  • CUDA (2007) made them programmable
  • TPUs & tensor cores (2016–17)
  • Cloud platforms: Earth Engine, Planetary Computer
All three were necessary. The 1986 back-prop algorithm already existed; what was missing was data at scale and hardware to multiply matrices quickly. Earth observation followed the same path a few years later once open archives and cloud computing met.

Deep learning is matrix multiplication — chips made it cheap

ChipDesignGood at
CPUFew powerful cores, big cachesBranchy, sequential code
GPUThousands of simple cores, SIMTParallel multiply–add on tensors
Tensor coreMatrix-multiply unit inside a GPUMixed precision (FP16/BF16/FP8)
TPUSystolic array (Google, 2015–16)Dense matmul at scale
One dense layer = one matmul: \(\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\). For a batch of 256 Sentinel-2 patches through ResNet-50 that is ≈ 1–2 TFLOP per forward pass — seconds on a CPU, milliseconds on a GPU.
2012: AlexNet trained on two consumer GTX 580 GPUs and won ImageNet by a wide margin — the moment the field switched.
The ResNet-50 number: about 4 G multiply–adds (≈ 8 GFLOP) per 224×224 image, so a batch of 256 is on the order of 1–2 TFLOP. Emphasise orders of magnitude, not exact marketing numbers.

Training compute grew ~10 orders of magnitude

  • Before 2010 compute followed Moore's law (doubling ≈ every 2 years).
  • In the deep-learning era it doubled roughly every ~6 months (Sevilla et al., 2022).
  • Scaling laws: loss falls as a power law of parameters, data and compute — the bet behind LLMs and Earth foundation models.
  • Cost now matters: carbon-aware training is one of the “ideal Earth FM” features (Part VIII).
