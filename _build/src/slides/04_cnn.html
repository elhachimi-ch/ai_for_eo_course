Hover a field: vegetation is dark in red (chlorophyll absorbs) and very bright in the near infrared — the red edge. An RGB camera never sees the NIR plateau; Sentinel-2 samples it with B5–B8A and the SWIR with B11–B12; a hyperspectral sensor records the whole curve, including mineral and water absorption features (grey bands = atmospheric water vapour). Switch the composite: false colour (NIR as red) makes vegetation red; SWIR separates bare soil and roofs; NDVI is a two-band formula. Every extra band is another channel of the tensor a model will read.

From colour to grayscale

RGB input
grayscale output Y
$$Y=w_R\,R+w_G\,G+w_B\,B,\qquad w_R+w_G+w_B=1$$$$Y_{601}=0.299\,R+0.587\,G+0.114\,B$$
BT.601 weights follow the eye: most sensitive to green, least to blue.

Same operation, many names

A weighted sum of the channels at every pixel is a 1 × 1 convolution. Spectral indices, PCA and the first layer of a CNN do the same thing — with physics-based or learned weights.

Panchromatic bands are physical grayscale: one broad band (Landsat 8/9 B8, 0.50–0.68 µm) at 15 m instead of 30 m.
Try “red only” vs “blue only”: the reservoir and river are dark in red and brighter in blue, fields swap contrast. The point to carry to CNNs: converting C channels into one number per pixel with fixed weights is a 1 × 1 convolution; a CNN simply learns those weights, and many sets of them at once.

Thresholding: grayscale → binary

input band / index
histogram · click or drag to set the threshold t
binary output B
$$B(i,j)=\begin{cases}1 & \text{if } I(i,j)>t\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$
Otsu: pick the \(t\) that best separates the two histogram classes $$t^\star=\arg\max_t\ \omega_0(t)\,\omega_1(t)\,\big[\mu_0(t)-\mu_1(t)\big]^2$$

EO

Water: \(\text{NDWI}=\frac{G-\text{NIR}}{G+\text{NIR}}>0\). A global threshold struggles with turbid water, shadows and mixed pixels — and it is a one-feature decision stump.

Start with NDWI: the histogram is clearly bimodal (land on the left, water on the right) and Otsu lands near 0, matching McFeeters' rule; the IoU against the true water mask is printed. Switch to grayscale and press Otsu: the histogram is not bimodal in a useful way, so the “optimal” threshold separates bright from dark, not one class from another. Thresholding is the simplest classifier — Part I called it a decision stump.

$$(\mathbf{I}\ast\mathbf{K})[i,j]=\sum_{m=0}^{2}\sum_{n=0}^{2}\mathbf{I}[iS{+}m{-}P,\ jS{+}n{-}P]\,\mathbf{K}[m,n]$$
Deep learning computes this cross-correlation (no kernel flip) — identical to convolution for symmetric kernels.

Multiply, add, slide

Each output is a dot product of the kernel with one window: large where the window looks like the kernel. Sobel fires on the field edge and the small bright building.

Press Step a few times and read the arithmetic under the grids: nine products, one sum, one output cell. Play animates the slide. Zero padding keeps the 8 × 8 size; stride 2 halves it — check the formula on the right. Positive responses are terracotta, negative blue. This is the operation a CNN layer repeats with dozens of learned kernels over all channels.

Filtering — kernels are feature detectors

input scene
feature map
$$(\mathbf{I}\ast\mathbf{K})_{i,j}=\sum_{c}\sum_{m,n}\mathbf{I}_{c,\,i+m,\,j+n}\,\mathbf{K}_{c,m,n}+b$$

Kernel

Median (non-linear): \(\hat I_{i,j}=\operatorname{median}\{I_{i+m,\,j+n}\}_{m,n\in\{-1,0,1\}}\) — removes salt-and-pepper noise and keeps edges, but is not a convolution.
Output size: \(\;W_{out}=\left\lfloor\frac{W-K+2P}{S}\right\rfloor+1\)
e.g. 64-px tile, K = 3, P = 1, S = 2 ⇒ 32 px.
In a CNN the kernel values are not hand-picked: they are weights learned by back-prop.
Click Sobel x / y: the road and field boundaries light up — the same edge features that classical remote sensing computed by hand. In a trained CNN the first layer learns filters very much like these, deeper layers combine them into textures and objects. Blur and Gaussian are low-pass filters (noise down, detail down); median is the non-linear cousin that kills salt-and-pepper noise — the input switches to a noisy copy to show it.

Application: filtering SAR speckle

speckled σ⁰ (dB)
filtered
looks L
$$I=R\cdot n,\qquad \mathbb{E}[n]=1,\ \ \operatorname{Var}[n]=\tfrac1L,\qquad \text{ENL}=\frac{\mu_I^2}{\sigma_I^2}$$
Lee filter — local statistics in the window: $$\hat R=\bar I+k\,(I-\bar I),\qquad k=\frac{1-C_u^2/C_I^2}{1+C_u^2},\quad C_u=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt L},\ \ C_I=\tfrac{\sigma_I}{\bar I}$$
flat field: \(C_I\approx C_u\Rightarrow k\to0\) (average) · edge or bright target: \(C_I\gg C_u\Rightarrow k\to1\) (keep)

Speckle is multiplicative

Echoes of many scatterers in one cell interfere. The mean blurs edges and bright targets; median and Lee adapt to local contrast. Sentinel-1 GRD ≈ 4–5 looks: filter, then threshold for flood maps — or let a CNN learn the despeckler.

Same scene, now as radar backscatter: water is dark (specular reflection away from the sensor), the town is bright (double bounce), fields are in between. With L = 1 the image is extremely grainy and the ENL of the dashed homogeneous field is ≈ 1. Tick the water mask: the raw image gives a salt-and-pepper mask (low IoU). Try mean 7×7 — ENL jumps, the mask cleans up, but the river edges and the bright town points blur. Lee keeps edges and point targets while still smoothing the fields. Larger L (multilooking) reduces speckle at the cost of resolution. Mean is a convolution; median and Lee are non-linear and adaptive — CNN despecklers (SAR-CNN) learn such behaviour from data.

Analysis-ready data

Surface reflectance, per-pixel quality flags, one map grid and full metadata, so that two dates or two sensors can be compared without more processing (CEOS CARD4L).

Easy to get wrong

  • Mixing L1C and L2A, or scale factors and offsets
  • Resampling categorical masks with bilinear interpolation
  • Misregistration of a few pixels between dates or sensors
  • Training on clear scenes, predicting on hazy ones
This is the part of an EO project that takes most of the time and that papers describe least. Sentinel-2 L2A from processing baseline 04.00 (January 2022) stores reflectance with an offset of −1000 (BOA_ADD_OFFSET): check the metadata before mixing old and new scenes. The SCL classes masked in the code are 0 no data, 3 cloud shadow, 8–9 cloud medium/high probability, 10 thin cirrus. STAC (SpatioTemporal Asset Catalog) is the common catalogue standard; the same code works on Microsoft Planetary Computer with its own URL and signing.

Cloud masks

Rules and thresholds (Fmask, Sen2Cor SCL) → gradient boosting (s2cloudless) → deep segmentation networks trained on CloudSEN12. Thin cloud edges and shadows are the hard part.

Try max NDVI: it removes clouds but keeps shadows, because darkening both bands leaves NDVI unchanged. Then add the shadow mask and the buffer.
Eight synthetic acquisitions of the same scene, each with its own clouds and their shadows offset to the south-east. Yellow is the detected cloud mask, purple the detected shadow. The latest date and the mean are ruined by clouds; the median already removes most of them because clouds are bright outliers. Masking clouds before the median helps, but shadows and thin cloud edges (below the detection threshold) remain: the residual-contamination number counts pixels still more than 0.02 off in any visible band. The 2-pixel buffer removes most of those edges. Push the cloudiness up: some pixels never get a clear look and become gaps — this is why monthly composites in the tropics are hard and why SAR matters.

Tiles, padding and normalisation

scene cut into tiles (dashed: what the model sees for one tile)
water map · differs from the whole-image result

The toy model

Smooth the standardised NDWI with a Gaussian (receptive field ±12 px, zero padding at the window edge), then threshold. A CNN behaves the same way near the tile border.

Keep an overlap \(m\ge r\), the receptive-field radius, and keep only the centre of each window: $$\text{window}=t+2m,\qquad \text{cost}\propto\Big(\tfrac{t+2m}{t}\Big)^2$$
Normalise with dataset statistics, never per tile: $$z_b=\frac{x_b-\mu_b}{\sigma_b},\quad \mu_b,\sigma_b\ \text{from the training set}$$
Large scenes never fit in GPU memory, so we predict tile by tile. With no overlap, each tile's border pixels only see zero padding on one side: the water bodies shrink at the seams (terracotta pixels). An overlap of at least the receptive-field radius (12 px here) makes the tiled result identical to the whole-image result, at a computing cost shown in the readout. Per-tile normalisation is a different bug: a tile with no water gets stretched until its brightest land pixels look like water. Use fixed statistics computed on the training data, and the same at inference.

Label noise

Wrong class, shifted polygon, or a label from another date. A few % of random noise is tolerated; systematic noise is learned as truth.

Imbalance

Rare classes (settlements, landslides, ships): stratified sampling, class weights, Dice or focal loss \(-(1-p_t)^\gamma\log p_t\).

Training labels can be collected anywhere. Test labels need a probability sample (random or stratified random), or accuracy and area estimates are biased.
Labels, not architectures, usually limit EO projects. The distinction in the callout carries into the evaluation block: a convenient, clustered training set is fine, but the accuracy you report must come from a probability sample of the map, otherwise you cannot generalise it to the whole area. Reusing an existing product as labels is legitimate for training but circular for testing against that same product.

Three priors that make images learnable

◎

Locality

A pixel is explained by its neighbours: a roof edge, a field boundary, a river bank. Each neuron looks at a small window (3×3).

⧉

Weight sharing

The same filter slides over the whole image. A “bright-line detector” is useful everywhere — so learn it once.

⇄

Equivariance

Shift the input, the feature map shifts the same way. A building is a building in any corner of the tile.

MLP on a 13×64×64 EuroSAT patch with 256 hidden units: \(13\cdot64\cdot64\cdot256 \approx\) 13.6 M weights in the first layer alone.
Conv layer, 64 filters of 3×3 over 13 bands: \(3\cdot3\cdot13\cdot64 =\) 7 488 weights — and it works on any image size.
The numbers make the point: convolution is a massive reduction in parameters by building in the right assumptions about images. 13·64·64 = 53 248 inputs × 256 = 13.6 M.

Receptive field

Each layer widens what a neuron “sees”. Two 3×3 convs see 5×5; with down-sampling a deep neuron covers hundreds of metres on Sentinel-2.

Pooling

Max/average pooling summarise a window: robustness to small shifts, cheaper compute. Global average pooling feeds the classifier.

Normalisation

Batch-norm stabilises training; with multispectral data, normalise inputs per band with dataset statistics.

Resolution matters in EO: a 3×3 kernel is 30 m on Sentinel-2 but 1.5 m on 50-cm imagery. Choose the backbone depth and input size for the size of the objects you want to see.
Highlight the EO-specific consideration: the same network “sees” very different ground areas depending on GSD. For informal settlements (small, dense roofs) you need sub-metre to ~3 m imagery or much deeper context.

Why ResNet matters

\(\partial\mathbf{y}/\partial\mathbf{x} = \partial\mathcal{F}/\partial\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{I}\): the “+I” keeps gradients alive through very deep nets — the same trick lives inside every Transformer block.

Why EO is harder than photos

Tiny objects (a car is 4 × 2 px at 50 cm), any orientation, dense packing in ports and car parks, scenes of 10 000 × 10 000 px that must be cut into tiles.

Box regression relative to an anchor \((x_a,y_a,w_a,h_a)\): $$t_x=\frac{x-x_a}{w_a},\quad t_y=\frac{y-y_a}{h_a},\quad t_w=\log\frac{w}{w_a},\quad t_h=\log\frac{h}{h_a}$$
Multi-task loss and the focal loss for the many easy background boxes: $$\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{cls}+\lambda\,[y\ge1]\,\mathcal{L}_{box},\qquad \mathrm{FL}(p_t)=-\alpha_t(1-p_t)^{\gamma}\log p_t$$
Overlap between a predicted box \(B_p\) and a true box \(B_g\): $$\mathrm{IoU}=\frac{|B_p\cap B_g|}{|B_p\cup B_g|}\in[0,1]$$
Detection outputs a variable-length list of (box, class, score). Classification asks “what is in this tile?”, segmentation “which class is each pixel?”, detection “where is each object, and how many are there?” — the question behind counting ships, aircraft, cars, solar panels or buildings. The focal loss down-weights the thousands of easy background boxes so the rare objects dominate the gradient. DETR removes hand-made anchors and NMS by matching predictions to objects one-to-one.

IoU, NMS and average precision

hover a box: IoU with the nearest ship
NMS: sort boxes by score; keep the best, delete every box with IoU > \(\tau\) with it; repeat. $$\mathrm{AP}=\int_0^1 p_{\text{interp}}(r)\,dr,\qquad \mathrm{mAP}=\tfrac1C\textstyle\sum_c\mathrm{AP}_c$$
COCO averages AP over IoU 0.50 : 0.05 : 0.95
Gold dashed boxes are the ships (ground truth). A detector fires several overlapping boxes on each ship: switch NMS off and the duplicates become false positives — precision and AP drop. A very low IoU threshold for NMS merges neighbouring ships moored side by side; a very high one keeps duplicates. The score slider moves the operating point (red dot) along the precision–recall curve; AP summarises the whole curve, independent of the threshold. Matching at IoU 0.75 instead of 0.5 demands tighter boxes and lowers AP. Hover any box to read its IoU.

Lab 2 · Transfer learning: ImageNet ResNet → EuroSAT

import torch, torch.nn as nn
from torchvision import datasets, transforms, models

tf = transforms.Compose([
    transforms.Resize(224), transforms.RandomHorizontalFlip(),
    transforms.RandomVerticalFlip(), transforms.ToTensor(),
    transforms.Normalize([0.485, 0.456, 0.406],
                         [0.229, 0.224, 0.225])])
ds = datasets.EuroSAT("data", transform=tf, download=True)
train_ds, val_ds = torch.utils.data.random_split(
    ds, [0.8, 0.2], generator=torch.Generator().manual_seed(42))

model = models.resnet18(weights=models.ResNet18_Weights.DEFAULT)
model.fc = nn.Linear(model.fc.in_features, 10)   # new head

# stage 1: freeze backbone, train the head (fast)
for n, p in model.named_parameters():
    p.requires_grad = n.startswith("fc")
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(filter(lambda p: p.requires_grad,
                               model.parameters()), lr=1e-3)
# stage 2: unfreeze all, lr=1e-4, a few more epochs

Why it works

Early ImageNet filters (edges, textures) are generic. Only the head is task-specific. With 27 000 EuroSAT patches, fine-tuning typically reaches high-90s % accuracy — far above the spectral MLP.

Lab 3 · U-Net semantic segmentation

  • Encoder (often a pre-trained ResNet) captures what; decoder recovers where.
  • Skips pass fine edges to the decoder ⇒ crisp field and building boundaries.
  • Loss: cross-entropy + Dice for rare classes; metric: IoU = TP / (TP + FP + FN).
import torch, torch.nn as nn
import segmentation_models_pytorch as smp
from torchgeo.datasets import LandCoverAI
from torch.utils.data import DataLoader

train = LandCoverAI("data", split="train", download=True)
dl = DataLoader(train, batch_size=8, shuffle=True)

# 5 classes: background, building, woodland, water, road
model = smp.Unet("resnet34", encoder_weights="imagenet",
                 in_channels=3, classes=5)
ce, dice = nn.CrossEntropyLoss(), smp.losses.DiceLoss("multiclass")
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4)

for batch in dl:
    x = batch["image"].float() / 255.0       # (B,3,512,512)
    y = batch["mask"].long()                 # (B,512,512)
    logits = model(x)                        # (B,5,512,512)
    loss = ce(logits, y) + dice(logits, y)
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

tp, fp, fn, tn = smp.metrics.get_stats(logits.argmax(1), y,
                     mode="multiclass", num_classes=5)
print("mIoU", smp.metrics.iou_score(tp, fp, fn, tn, reduction="macro"))
U-Net is the default architecture for EO segmentation and the decoder pattern reappears on top of foundation models (UPerNet / U-Net decoders in TerraTorch). LandCover.ai has 25–50 cm aerial orthophotos of Poland; buildings and roads are the hard, small classes.

Data & models

SEN12MS, BigEarthNet-MM, SEN12MS-CR (cloud removal), Sen1Floods11 · multimodal encoders CROMA, DOFA, TerraMind.

The two sensors fail in different conditions, which is why fusing them helps. Early fusion stacks co-registered bands into one input tensor; mid fusion gives each sensor its own encoder (they have different statistics) and merges features; late fusion combines the predictions and can drop a sensor when it is unavailable — for example optical under clouds. Multimodal foundation models (Part VIII) learn a shared representation from many paired SAR–optical acquisitions.

Mapping under clouds with SAR

optical, false colour 11·8·4
SAR VV backscatter (dB)
map · water · veg. · soil · built-up
Late fusion of conditionally independent sensors: $$p(y\mid\mathbf{x}_o,\mathbf{x}_s)\propto p(y)\,p(\mathbf{x}_o\mid y)\,p(\mathbf{x}_s\mid y)$$ under a cloud, drop the optical term: $$p(y\mid\mathbf{x}_s)\propto p(y)\,p(\mathbf{x}_s\mid y)$$

What to look for

Optical alone fails under clouds; SAR alone confuses soil and vegetation; stacking without a mask trusts cloudy pixels. Fusion with a mask keeps the best of both.

Same synthetic scene, with clouds over the optical image and a 4-look speckled VV/VH radar image. One classifier (Gaussian naive Bayes) is trained on clear-sky pixels. With “optical, clouds masked” the map has holes (hatched); “SAR only” fills everything but mixes bare soil and sparse vegetation; “stacked fusion” is excellent under clear sky but feeds cloud reflectance into the classifier. “Fusion, clouds masked” uses both sensors where the sky is clear and SAR alone under clouds. Raise the cloud cover and compare the accuracy under cloud. With naive Bayes, early and late fusion coincide on clear pixels; with deep networks they differ, and mid fusion usually wins.

Spatial autocorrelation: why random splits flatter you

crop parcels · dots = labelled pixels, coloured by CV fold

“Near things are more related than distant things” (Tobler). Moran's \(I\): $$I=\frac{n}{\sum_{ij}w_{ij}}\,\frac{\sum_{ij}w_{ij}(x_i-\bar x)(x_j-\bar x)}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}$$

Choose the split for the question

Spatial blocks or leave-region-out when the map extends beyond the sampled area. A random probability sample of the map makes ordinary CV unbiased (Wadoux et al. 2021).

Each parcel has its own spectral “signature” (soil, sowing date, management): the parcel effect slider. The labels come from a field survey that visited three areas. Random 5-fold CV puts pixels of the same parcel in training and test, so a nearest-neighbour classifier simply recognises the parcel: accuracy is inflated (terracotta bar). Spatial-block CV holds out whole blocks and comes close to the true accuracy on parcels nobody visited (green bar). Set the parcel effect to 0 and the gap disappears; switch to a random sample of the whole map and random CV becomes honest again — the point made by Wadoux et al. (2021). The distance readout shows why: random-CV test pixels sit right next to training pixels.

Good practice

Stratified random sample, reference labels from better data, error matrix in area proportions, area ± 95 % CI. Pixel counting is biased whenever commission ≠ omission.

The map over-maps change (commission errors), so pixel counting overestimates the area. A stratified random sample checked against reference data (here: the truth) gives an unbiased estimate with a confidence interval. Repeat 100×: about 95 % of the intervals cover the true area — that is what “95 % confidence” means. Equal allocation puts more points in the small “change” stratum and usually gives a tighter interval than proportional allocation. Move the slider to omission: now pixel counting underestimates. This is the approach recommended in the GFOI methods guidance for REDD+ reporting.

An evaluation protocol reviewers will trust

Checklist

  1. Say what the map is for and which error matters most (missed vs false).
  2. Split by space, time or region, matched to how the model will be used; no leakage through tiles, dates or normalisation.
  3. Compare with simple baselines (thresholds, random forest) and an existing product.
  4. Report the confusion matrix and per-class F1 / IoU, not only OA or kappa; mAP for detection; RMSE + bias for regression.
  5. Give uncertainty: CIs by bootstrap or stratified estimators; area ± CI.
  6. Map the errors and say where the model should not be used.
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold, GroupKFold, cross_val_score
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier

# X: features, y: labels, xs/ys: pixel coordinates in metres
block = (xs // 5000).astype(int) * 100_000 + (ys // 5000).astype(int)  # 5-km blocks
rf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, n_jobs=-1, random_state=0)

rand = cross_val_score(rf, X, y, cv=KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0))
spat = cross_val_score(rf, X, y, cv=GroupKFold(5), groups=block)
print(f"random CV {rand.mean():.3f}  ·  spatial CV {spat.mean():.3f}")
A large gap between the two numbers means your labels are spatially clustered and the random-CV accuracy should not be reported as map accuracy.
Leakage is the most common flaw in applied ML papers (Kapoor & Narayanan 2023 list it across 17 fields). In EO it enters through overlapping tiles in train and test, the same field on different dates, or normalisation statistics computed on all data. The block size should be at least the range of spatial autocorrelation of the residuals (a variogram tells you). GroupKFold does not shuffle the groups; GroupKFold(shuffle=True) exists in scikit-learn ≥ 1.6, or use StratifiedGroupKFold.
