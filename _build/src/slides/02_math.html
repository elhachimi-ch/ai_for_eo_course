Training = minimising a loss

Empirical risk minimisation (with regularisation):
$$\hat{\theta} \;=\; \arg\min_{\theta}\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell\big(f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i),\,y_i\big) \;+\; \lambda\,\Omega(\theta)$$
TaskLoss \(\ell\)EO example
RegressionMSE \((\hat y-y)^2\), MAEETo, biomass, soil moisture
ClassificationCross-entropy \(-\log p_{y}\)Land cover, crop type
SegmentationCE + Dice / focalBuildings, floods, slums
GenerationAdversarial, L1, diffusionSAR→optical, cloud removal

Generalisation, not memorisation

We minimise the loss on training data but care about unseen places and dates. Regularisers \(\Omega\) (weight decay), dropout, augmentation and early stopping fight over-fitting.

EO trap · spatial autocorrelation

Neighbouring pixels are near-duplicates. Random train/test splits leak information and inflate accuracy. Split by blocks, polygons, tiles or cities (spatial cross-validation).

The class imbalance in EO (e.g., 2% slum pixels) motivates Dice/focal losses in segmentation. Spatial CV will come back in the informal-settlement case study as leave-one-city-out evaluation.

Gradient descent — walk downhill

$$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\,\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t)$$
  • Learning rate η: too small = slow, too big = divergence or oscillation. Try it →
  • Stochastic GD: estimate \(\nabla\mathcal{L}\) on a mini-batch of 32–512 patches; noise helps escape poor minima.
  • Momentum / Adam: average past gradients and rescale per parameter — the default optimisers today (AdamW for Transformers).
  • Non-convex loss: start point and η decide which valley you land in.
Live demo: with η = 0.3 from θ₀ = 2.6 the ball reaches the right-hand minimum. Push η to about 1.2+ and it oscillates or jumps valleys. Start at −2.8 to land in the deeper left minimum. This is the whole of training, in one dimension.

Back-propagation = the chain rule, applied backwards

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y}\cdot\frac{\partial \hat y}{\partial z}\cdot\frac{\partial z}{\partial w} = 2(\hat y - y)\,\sigma(z)\big(1-\sigma(z)\big)\,x$$
  • Forward pass computes and caches every intermediate value.
  • Backward pass multiplies local derivatives from the loss back to each weight — one sweep gives all gradients at ≈ the cost of one forward pass.
  • PyTorch/JAX do this automatically (autograd): you write the forward pass, loss.backward() does the rest.
  • Products of many small factors (σ′ ≤ 0.25) ⇒ vanishing gradients in deep or recurrent nets — the motivation for ReLU, ResNets and LSTMs.
Walk the dashed red arrows right to left. The final bullet sets up three later stories: ReLU (Part III), residual connections (Part IV) and LSTM gates (Part V) are all fixes for the vanishing-gradient problem.

Why cross-entropy? Maximum likelihood

Softmax turns scores (logits) into class probabilities:
$$p_k(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}}$$
Maximising the likelihood of the labels = minimising cross-entropy:
$$\arg\max_\theta \prod_i p_\theta(y_i\mid\mathbf{x}_i) \;=\; \arg\min_\theta \; -\sum_i \log p_\theta(y_i\mid\mathbf{x}_i)$$

MSE ⇔ Gaussian noise likelihood; cross-entropy ⇔ categorical likelihood. Choosing a loss is choosing a noise model.
