The paradigm

Hand-designed features → a shallow model with a clean objective (often convex) and guarantees from statistical learning theory: control the capacity, not just the training error.

In remote sensing

Gaussian maximum-likelihood classifier (1970s–90s) → SVM for hyperspectral data (2000s) → random forest as the default in Earth Engine (2010s).

Still alive

On tabular features, tuned tree ensembles still match or beat deep nets (Grinsztajn et al., 2022). Cheap, strong baselines for every EO project.

The shaded band marks the classical era (mid-1980s to the deep-learning switch around 2012). The five families on the next slides are the ones you will meet in every EO paper and in Earth Engine: naive Bayes / maximum likelihood, decision trees, bagging and random forests, boosting, SVM. Training = minimising a loss

Empirical risk minimisation (with regularisation):
$$\hat{\theta} \;=\; \arg\min_{\theta}\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell\big(f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i),\,y_i\big) \;+\; \lambda\,\Omega(\theta)$$
TaskLoss \(\ell\)EO example
RegressionMSE \((\hat y-y)^2\), MAEETo, biomass, soil moisture
ClassificationCross-entropy \(-\log p_{y}\)Land cover, crop type
SegmentationCE + Dice / focalBuildings, floods, slums
GenerationAdversarial, L1, diffusionSAR→optical, cloud removal

Generalisation, not memorisation

We minimise the loss on training data but care about unseen places and dates. Regularisers \(\Omega\) (weight decay), dropout, augmentation and early stopping fight over-fitting.

EO trap · spatial autocorrelation

Neighbouring pixels are near-duplicates. Random train/test splits leak information and inflate accuracy. Split by blocks, polygons, tiles or cities (spatial cross-validation).

The class imbalance in EO (e.g., 2% slum pixels) motivates Dice/focal losses in segmentation. Spatial cross-validation gets its own slides, with a live demo, at the end of this part.

Why cross-entropy? Maximum likelihood

Softmax turns scores (logits) into class probabilities:
$$p_k(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} e^{z_j}}$$
Maximising the likelihood of the labels = minimising cross-entropy:
$$\arg\max_\theta \prod_i p_\theta(y_i\mid\mathbf{x}_i) \;=\; \arg\min_\theta \; -\sum_i \log p_\theta(y_i\mid\mathbf{x}_i)$$

Fitting is not learning: bias, variance and regularisation

A linear model in basis functions, fitted by ridge regression: $$\hat y(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}w_k\,\phi_k(t),\qquad \hat{\mathbf{w}}=(\Phi^\top\Phi+\lambda I)^{-1}\Phi^\top\mathbf{y}$$ Expected error on a new date splits into three parts: $$\mathbb{E}\big[(y-\hat y)^2\big]=\underbrace{\mathrm{Bias}^2}_{\text{too simple}}+\underbrace{\mathrm{Var}}_{\text{too flexible}}+\underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}$$

Complexity is chosen on held-out data

Training error always falls as the model grows; held-out error falls, then rises. Pick the degree, tree depth or λ where held-out error is lowest — in EO, held out in space. Forests attack variance; boosting attacks bias.

An NDVI season sampled on a few cloud-free dates — the classic phenology-fitting problem. Degree 0–1 under-fits (high bias: the curve cannot follow green-up and senescence); degree 10–12 with few dates over-fits (high variance: the grey refits on new data disagree wildly between dates). Right panel: training error keeps falling with degree, test error is U-shaped; the dotted line is the noise floor σ that no model can beat. Raise λ at degree 12: ridge shrinks the weights and the test error comes back down — regularisation trades a little bias for a lot less variance. Add more dates and the U flattens: more data lets you afford a more flexible model. With only 8 dates, push the degree past 8: test error falls again, because once there are more coefficients than dates the (almost un-regularised) solver picks the smoothest interpolating curve — a small-scale double descent (Belkin et al. 2019), the regime deep networks live in (Part V). Dates are drawn one per slice of the season, so there are no long cloudy gaps; with real gaps, high degrees extrapolate even more wildly. This decomposition explains the classical learners that follow: bagging and random forests attack variance, boosting attacks bias.

Naive Bayes — classify by probability

click adds:
$$P(k\mid\mathbf{x})=\frac{P(k)\,p(\mathbf{x}\mid k)}{\sum_{k'}P(k')\,p(\mathbf{x}\mid k')}\;\overset{\text{naive}}{\propto}\;P(k)\prod_{j=1}^{d}p(x_j\mid k)$$ $$p(x_j\mid k)=\mathcal{N}\big(x_j;\,\mu_{kj},\sigma^2_{kj}\big),\qquad \hat y=\arg\max_k\Big[\log P(k)+\sum_j\log p(x_j\mid k)\Big]$$

“Naive” = bands independent given the class

Each class is a product of 1-D Gaussians, so its ellipses stay aligned with the axes. Training = means and variances per class: one pass, works with a handful of pixels.

EO classic · maximum-likelihood classifier

Keep the full covariance: \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid k)\propto|\Sigma_k|^{-1/2}e^{-\frac12(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top\Sigma_k^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu_k)}\). Ellipses tilt along the soil line; boundaries become quadratic. The standard supervised classifier of remote sensing for decades.

Demo: start in naive mode — the soil / built-up cloud is elongated along the soil line, but the naive ellipses cannot tilt, so they spread probability over empty space and bulge towards the vegetation cloud. Switch to full Σ: the ellipses rotate onto the soil line and the boundary bends (dark rings mark misclassified pixels). Add a few soil pixels near the vegetation tip to see the two models disagree. Hover anywhere to read the three posteriors; click to add training pixels of the selected class and watch the class prior and the boundary move. Note that correlated bands violate the naive assumption, which makes the probabilities over-confident even when the argmax is right (Domingos & Pazzani 1997).

Decision trees — learned IF–THEN rules

$$\text{Gini}(S)=1-\sum_k p_k^2\qquad H(S)=-\sum_k p_k\log_2 p_k$$ $$\Delta I(j,t)=I(S)-\tfrac{|S_L|}{|S|}\,I(S_L)-\tfrac{|S_R|}{|S|}\,I(S_R)$$
Greedy: at every node try each band \(j\) and threshold \(t\) (split \(x_j\le t\)); keep the largest impurity drop \(\Delta I\); recurse.

Bagging & random forests — many noisy trees, one calm vote



  

    

      
      

        
        
        
      

      

    

    

      

        $$\mathbb{E}\big[(y-\hat f)^2\big]=\text{Bias}^2+\text{Variance}+\sigma^2_\varepsilon$$
        $$\hat f_{\text{bag}}(\mathbf{x})=\operatorname{vote}\{T_b(\mathbf{x})\}_{b=1}^{B},\qquad \operatorname{Var}\big[\bar T\big]=\rho\,\sigma^2+\frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2$$
      

      
Two sources of randomness
    
        
  • Bootstrap: each tree sees \(n\) pixels drawn with replacement; averaging \(B\) trees shrinks the \(\tfrac{1-\rho}{B}\) term.
    • 
        
  • Random features: each split tries only \(m\approx\sqrt{p}\) bands, so trees decorrelate and \(\rho\) drops.
    • 
        
  • Out-of-bag: \((1-\tfrac1n)^n\to e^{-1}\approx\) 37 % of pixels are unused by each tree — a free validation set.

      
EO workhorse (ee.Classifier.smileRandomForest): robust to label noise and to 100+ stacked features. Prefer permutation importance — impurity importance is biased.

    

  




Demo: “1 deep tree” shows the jagged, over-fitted map from the previous slide. Switch to Bagging and slide B from 1 to 100: the islands melt away, the colour intensity (vote share) shows where the forest is unsure, and test accuracy rises. Random forest adds feature subsampling (here one random band per split, because we only have two). OOB accuracy tracks test accuracy without any held-out data. Bias is unchanged — that is what boosting attacks next.







Boosting I · AdaBoost — learn from your mistakes



  

    

      
      

        
        
        
        
        
      

      

    

    

      

        $$\varepsilon_t=\sum_i w_i\,\mathbb{1}\big[h_t(\mathbf{x}_i)\neq y_i\big],\qquad \alpha_t=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}$$
        $$w_i\leftarrow\frac{w_i\,e^{-\alpha_t\,y_i\,h_t(\mathbf{x}_i)}}{Z_t},\qquad H(\mathbf{x})=\operatorname{sign}\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_t\,h_t(\mathbf{x})\Big)$$
      

      
Sequential, not parallel
Each weak learner — a one-split stump (dashed gold line) — trains on re-weighted pixels: the ones the ensemble still gets wrong grow bigger. AdaBoost is stage-wise minimisation of the exponential loss \(e^{-yF(\mathbf{x})}\).

      
Try it
Seasons (XOR): single-season crops vs bare land and orchards in (NDVI March, NDVI July). A sum of stumps uses one band at a time and cannot model the interaction — switch to depth-2 trees. Bagging cuts variance; boosting cuts bias, but it chases mislabelled pixels.

    

  




Demo: on the ring, press +1 a few times. Each stump is a single threshold; the misclassified pixels swell; α is large when ε is small. Press +25: the staircase boundary wraps the ring. Then Seasons (XOR) with stumps — training error stays high because an additive model of single-band rules cannot express “high in March XOR high in July”. Switch to depth-2 trees and it is solved in a few rounds. In practice, boosting on noisy reference polygons can over-fit the label noise; gradient boosting with shrinkage and early stopping is the modern form.







Boosting II · Gradient boosting — fit the residuals, step by step



  

    

      
      

        
        
        
        
      

      

    

    

      

        $$r_i^{(m)}=-\left[\frac{\partial\,\ell\big(y_i,F(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)}{\partial F(\mathbf{x}_i)}\right]_{F=F_{m-1}}$$
        $$h_m=\arg\min_h\sum_i\big(r_i^{(m)}-h(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)^2,\qquad F_m=F_{m-1}+\nu\,h_m$$
        
Squared loss ⇒ \(r_i=y_i-F(\mathbf{x}_i)\): literally fit the residuals. Absolute loss ⇒ \(r_i=\operatorname{sign}(y_i-F)\): cloudy dips stop pulling the curve down.

      

      
XGBoost · LightGBM · CatBoost
Newton (second-order) steps and regularised leaves, \(\mathcal{L}=\sum_i\ell(y_i,\hat y_i)+\sum_k\big(\gamma T_k+\tfrac12\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}_k\rVert^2\big)\); histogram splits, missing-value handling, GPU training.

      
The go-to model for tabular EO regression: yield, ET, soil moisture, biomass from bands, weather and terrain features.

    

  




Demo: each click fits a small tree (gold, bottom panel) to the current residuals (blue bars) and adds ν times it to the model (terracotta). With ν = 1 it fits fast but chases noise; ν = 0.1–0.3 needs more trees and generalises better (shrinkage). Now look at the cloud dips: with squared loss the curve sags towards them; switch to absolute loss — the gradient is only the sign of the residual, so outliers have bounded influence. The loss you choose is the gradient you fit: that is the whole idea of gradient boosting, and of deep learning too.







Support vector machines — the widest street



  

    

      
      

        
        
        
        
        
      

      

    

    

      

        $$\min_{\mathbf{w},b,\boldsymbol\xi}\ \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2+C\sum_i\xi_i\quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i+b)\ge1-\xi_i,\ \ \xi_i\ge0$$
        
margin width \(=2/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\) · same as hinge loss \(\sum_i\max\big(0,1-y_if(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)+\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2\)

        $$f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i\in\text{SV}}\alpha_i\,y_i\,K(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x})+b,\qquad K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')=e^{-\gamma\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rVert^2}$$
      

      
Kernel trick & sparsity
Only the support vectors (gold rings) define the boundary. A kernel computes dot products in a high-dimensional feature space without building it. Small \(C\): wide, tolerant margin; large \(C\) and \(\gamma\): tight, wiggly fit.

      
SVMs ruled hyperspectral classification in the 2000s: hundreds of bands, few labelled pixels — where the margin principle shines. Scale features first.

    

  




Demo: linear kernel on water vs vegetation — solid line f = 0, dashed lines f = ±1, the light band between them is the margin; only the ringed points matter (delete any other point and nothing changes). Lower C: the margin widens and more points become support vectors. Switch to Seasons (XOR) — no line works; RBF kernel solves it. Push γ to the maximum and C high: islands around single pixels = over-fitting. Click to add vegetation pixels, shift-click (or right-click) for the other class. The optimiser running in the browser is Platt's simplified SMO.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import GroupKFold, cross_val_score

# X_bands: (n_pixels, 10) Sentinel-2 L2A reflectances
# y: land-cover label, groups: id of the polygon each pixel came from
def indices(X):
    B2, B3, B4, B8, B11 = X[:, 0], X[:, 1], X[:, 2], X[:, 6], X[:, 8]
    ndvi = (B8 - B4) / (B8 + B4 + 1e-6)
    ndwi = (B3 - B8) / (B3 + B8 + 1e-6)
    ndbi = (B11 - B8) / (B11 + B8 + 1e-6)   # built-up
    return np.c_[X, ndvi, ndwi, ndbi]

F = indices(X_bands)
rf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, n_jobs=-1,
                            class_weight="balanced")
# group-wise CV: pixels of one polygon never in train AND test
scores = cross_val_score(rf, F, y, groups=groups,
                         cv=GroupKFold(5), scoring="f1_macro")
print(f"macro-F1 = {scores.mean():.3f} ± {scores.std():.3f}")

Cloud masks
Rules and thresholds (Fmask, Sen2Cor SCL) → gradient boosting (s2cloudless) → deep segmentation networks trained on CloudSEN12. Thin cloud edges and shadows are the hard part.

      
Try max NDVI: it removes clouds but keeps shadows, because darkening both bands leaves NDVI unchanged. Then add the shadow mask and the buffer.

    

  




Eight synthetic acquisitions of the same scene, each with its own clouds and their shadows offset to the south-east. Yellow is the detected cloud mask, purple the detected shadow. The latest date and the mean are ruined by clouds; the median already removes most of them because clouds are bright outliers. Label noise
Wrong class, shifted polygon, or a label from another date. A few % of random noise is tolerated; systematic noise is learned as truth.

      
Imbalance
Rare classes (settlements, landslides, ships): stratified sampling, class weights, Dice or focal loss \(-(1-p_t)^\gamma\log p_t\).

      
Training labels can be collected anywhere. Test labels need a probability sample (random or stratified random), or accuracy and area estimates are biased.

    

  




Labels, not architectures, usually limit EO projects. The distinction in the callout carries into the evaluation block: a convenient, clustered training set is fine, but the accuracy you report must come from a probability sample of the map, otherwise you cannot generalise it to the whole area. Reusing an existing product as labels is legitimate for training but circular for testing against that same product.







Spatial autocorrelation: why random splits flatter you



  

    

      

        
crop parcels · dots = labelled pixels, coloured by CV fold

        
accuracy of the same 5-NN classifier

      

      

        
        
        
        
      

      

        
        
      

      

    

    

      
“Near things are more related than distant things” (Tobler). Moran's \(I\):
        $$I=\frac{n}{\sum_{ij}w_{ij}}\,\frac{\sum_{ij}w_{ij}(x_i-\bar x)(x_j-\bar x)}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}$$

      
Choose the split for the question
Spatial blocks or leave-region-out when the map extends beyond the sampled area. A random probability sample of the map makes ordinary CV unbiased (Wadoux et al. 2021).

    

  




Each parcel has its own spectral “signature” (soil, sowing date, management): the parcel effect slider. The labels come from a field survey that visited three areas. Random 5-fold CV puts pixels of the same parcel in training and test, so a nearest-neighbour classifier simply recognises the parcel: accuracy is inflated (terracotta bar). Spatial-block CV holds out whole blocks and comes close to the true accuracy on parcels nobody visited (green bar). Set the parcel effect to 0 and the gap disappears; switch to a random sample of the whole map and random CV becomes honest again — the point made by Wadoux et al. (2021). The distance readout shows why: random-CV test pixels sit right next to training pixels.

Area estimation: count the sample, not the pixels



  

    

      

        
change map · dots = reference sample (white = true change)

        
estimates of the change area

      

      

        
        
        
      

      

        
        
      

      

    

    

      
Strata \(h\) = map classes, weights \(W_h\), sample proportion \(\hat p_{h1}\) of true change in stratum \(h\):
        $$\hat A=A_{\text{tot}}\sum_h W_h\,\hat p_{h1}$$
        $$\widehat{SE}(\hat A)=A_{\text{tot}}\sqrt{\sum_h W_h^2\,\frac{\hat p_{h1}(1-\hat p_{h1})}{n_h-1}}$$

      
Good practice
Stratified random sample, reference labels from better data, error matrix in area proportions, area ± 95 % CI. Pixel counting is biased whenever commission ≠ omission.

    

  




The map over-maps change (commission errors), so pixel counting overestimates the area. A stratified random sample checked against reference data (here: the truth) gives an unbiased estimate with a confidence interval. Repeat 100×: about 95 % of the intervals cover the true area — that is what “95 % confidence” means. Equal allocation puts more points in the small “change” stratum and usually gives a tighter interval than proportional allocation. Move the slider to omission: now pixel counting underestimates. This is the approach recommended in the GFOI methods guidance for REDD+ reporting.







An evaluation protocol reviewers will trust



  

    

      
Checklist
    
        
  1. Say what the map is for and which error matters most (missed vs false).
    2. 
        
  2. Split by space, time or region, matched to how the model will be used; no leakage through tiles, dates or normalisation.
    3. 
        
  3. Compare with simple baselines (thresholds, random forest) and an existing product.
    4. 
        
  4. Report the confusion matrix and per-class F1 / IoU, not only OA or kappa; mAP for detection; RMSE + bias for regression.
    5. 
        
  5. Give uncertainty: CIs by bootstrap or stratified estimators; area ± CI.

rand = cross_val_score(rf, X, y, cv=KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0))

print(f"random CV {rand.mean():.3f}  ·  spatial CV {spat.mean():.3f}")


      
A large gap between the two numbers means your labels are spatially clustered and the random-CV accuracy should not be reported as map accuracy.

    

  




Leakage is the most common flaw in applied ML papers (Kapoor & Narayanan 2023 list it across 17 fields). In EO it enters through overlapping tiles in train and test, the same field on different dates, or normalisation statistics computed on all data. The block size should be at least the range of spatial autocorrelation of the residuals (a variogram tells you). GroupKFold does not shuffle the groups; GroupKFold(shuffle=True) exists in scikit-learn ≥ 1.6, or use StratifiedGroupKFold.

The notebook defines best_day (all tiles of the clearest date), sample_polygons (same-class 7 × 7-pixel patches around random seeds, the way an analyst digitises training polygons) and the band list. The headline result: random folds put pixels of the same polygon on both sides of the split, so every model looks better than it is. In our run (20 June 2021) the random forest scored 0.79 macro-F1 with random folds and 0.68 with 1 km spatial blocks; flexible models (k-NN, forests, boosting) gain 0.10–0.13, naive Bayes only 0.03; and the ranking flips — boosting wins under random folds, the SVM under spatial blocks. With isolated random pixels instead of patches the gap almost vanishes (≤ 0.02): the leak comes from how the labels were collected.
