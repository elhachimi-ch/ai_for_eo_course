What is a foundation model?

Definition

A model pre-trained on massive datasets with self-supervised objectives and adaptable to many downstream tasks.

  1. Massive EO archive — petabytes of unlabelled imagery
  2. Self-supervised pre-training — masking, contrast, distillation
  3. General geospatial representation — one encoder
  4. Fine-tuning → many downstream tasks

Four ways to learn without labels

Reconstruction · MAE, SatMAE

Mask ~75% of patches, reconstruct pixels → fine spatial detail.
\(\mathcal{L}=\tfrac{1}{|M|}\sum_{p\in M}\lVert \hat{\mathbf{x}}_p-\mathbf{x}_p\rVert^2\)

Contrastive · CLIP, RemoteCLIP

Pull matching pairs together, push others apart → semantics.
\(\mathcal{L}=-\log\frac{e^{\mathrm{sim}(z_i,z_i^+)/\tau}}{\sum_j e^{\mathrm{sim}(z_i,z_j)/\tau}}\)

Distillation · DINO, DINOv2

Student matches a momentum teacher on different crops/views → strong semantic features, no negatives.

Predictive · JEPA

Predict the latent representation of masked regions, not pixels → abstract “world-model” features.

In EO a natural “positive pair” is the same place seen by two sensors or on two dates — free supervision from geography.
Four families from the seminar deck, with the defining loss for the two simplest. The EO-specific twist on contrastive learning (SeCo, CROMA, DeCUR) is to use co-located multi-sensor or multi-temporal views as positives.

Why JEPA for EO?

Earth observation is full of clouds, noise, missing observations and multi-sensor inconsistencies. Spending capacity on reconstructing every noisy pixel is wasteful; predicting representations may be more meaningful.

JEPA objective (schematic): \(\;\mathcal{L}=\big\lVert\, g_\phi\big(s_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{\text{context}}),\,\text{pos}\big)-\operatorname{sg}\!\big[\bar{s}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{target}})\big]\big\rVert^2\)
context encoder \(s_\theta\), predictor \(g_\phi\), EMA target encoder \(\bar{s}\), stop-gradient sg.
From the seminar deck. Open question for discussion: does predicting in latent space make models more robust to clouds and sensor artefacts in practice? This is an active research area.

GeoFMs are not yet Earth-system models

1 · Physics ignorance

Most GeoFMs learn statistical correlations (higher NDVI ↔ higher biomass), not photosynthesis, water balance, energy balance or radiative transfer.

2 · EO treated as computer vision

Methods inherited from CV — image reconstruction, contrastive learning, object recognition. But EO is not photographs: it is time series, spatio-temporal, multi-resolution, multimodal, physical signal.

3 · Weak interpretability

Hard to explain predictions and hard to relate embeddings to physical processes.

4 · Poor transferability

Performance drops across climate zones, ecosystems and continents — exactly the shift Africa and the Global South face.

Research direction: from “Can we learn representations from Earth observations?” to “Can we learn physically meaningful representations of Earth-system processes?”
Four limitations exactly as in the seminar deck. The fourth point matters for Moroccan and African applications: models pre-trained mostly on North-American/European data transfer poorly — a reason to fine-tune and to validate locally.

Research example: AtlasGeoFM — physics-aware GeoFM

Idea

Embed high-level models of balances (energy, water cycle) and time-series dynamics into representation learning.

Inputs

ERA5-Land meteorology + PM-ETo, with lat/lon and day-of-year context.

Training

Pre-train an encoder-only Transformer (MLM-like masking), then fine-tune: ETo nowcasting, gap-filling, anomaly detection. Benchmarked against semi-physical models and ML.

This is the instructor's own research direction (from the seminar deck) and links back to Lab 4: the LSTM ETo forecaster is the small, supervised version; AtlasGeoFM is the self-supervised, physics-aware, multi-task version.

Four ways to put physics into machine learning

①

Physics as input

Indices, reference ET (FAO-56), terrain, solar geometry as features or priors. Cheap and robust.

②

Emulate & invert

Simulate a radiative-transfer model (PROSAIL), train ML on the simulations, apply to real pixels: hybrid retrieval. The SNAP biophysical processor is such a network.

③

Physics in the loss

Penalise violations of conservation laws, monotonicity or a PDE residual — physics-informed neural networks.

④

Hybrid models

Keep the physical model, learn only the unknown part (a parameterisation) end-to-end: NeuralGCM.

Physics-constrained training: $$\mathcal{L}=\underbrace{\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i\big(y_i-f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_i)\big)^2}_{\text{fit the data}}+\lambda\,\underbrace{\big\lVert\mathcal{N}[f_\theta]\big\rVert^2}_{\text{obey the physics}}$$
The aim (Reichstein et al. 2019): models that fit the data, stay physically consistent, extrapolate better to new conditions, and can be interpreted in terms of processes.
This picks up the first limitation of current GeoFMs: physics ignorance. The four options go from the least to the most invasive. Option ② is the workhorse of operational biophysical products: Copernicus LAI, fAPAR and fCover from Sentinel-2 come from neural networks trained on PROSAIL simulations. Option ③ needs a differentiable physical constraint, e.g. water-balance closure or energy-balance closure in ET models. Option ④ is where weather and climate modelling is heading.

Retrieving LAI: empirical, LUT or hybrid

forward model: canopy reflectance vs LAI
retrieval on 260 test pixels
Toy canopy model, band \(b\), soil brightness \(s\): $$\rho_b=\rho_{\infty,b}+\big(s\,\rho_{s,b}-\rho_{\infty,b}\big)\,e^{-k_b\,\mathrm{LAI}}$$
LUT inversion: average the LAI of the \(k\) simulations closest to the observation $$\widehat{\mathrm{LAI}}=\tfrac1k\textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_k(\boldsymbol\rho)}\mathrm{LAI}_j$$

Hybrid = physics + ML

Train a regressor on (simulated reflectance + noise → LAI). Physics supplies unlimited labels; ML supplies speed.

The empirical model is a cubic NDVI → LAI fit on 25 field plots collected on one soil type: fine on that soil, biased on brighter or darker soils, and it saturates at high LAI. The look-up table (3 000 simulations over all soils) handles soil variation because the physics knows about soil; the hybrid model learns the same inverse mapping from 1 500 simulations and runs in microseconds per pixel — this is how the Sentinel-2 biophysical processor works with PROSAIL. Set the soil range to 0 and the empirical fit becomes competitive; raise the noise and all methods degrade, LUT/hybrid most gracefully. The canopy model here is a deliberately simple two-band Beer–Lambert form, not PROSAIL.

Four ways to use a foundation model

①

Embeddings

Freeze, extract vectors, train a light model (RF, k-NN, MLP). No GPU needed. e.g. AlphaEarth: 64-D, unit-length, 10 m, annual.

②

Linear probe

Freeze the encoder, train one linear layer — the standard test of representation quality.

③

PEFT / LoRA

Train small adapters, keep 99% of weights frozen: \(\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}_0+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\), rank \(r\ll d\).

④

Full fine-tune

Encoder + decoder (U-Net/UPerNet) end-to-end. Best accuracy with enough labels and GPU.

Decision rule: few labels & no GPU → ①; benchmark a model → ②; moderate labels, limited GPU → ③; many labels, pixel-precise task → ④. Always compare with a CNN baseline.
LoRA: with d = 1024 and r = 8, an adapter for one weight matrix has 2·1024·8 = 16 k parameters instead of 1 M. The same PEFT idea is used to adapt LLMs (Part X).

Tabular foundation models: learn a table in one forward pass

Most EO prediction is tabular

Rows: field plots, parcels, stations, labelled pixels. Columns: bands, indices, SAR, DEM, climate, or a 64-D AlphaEarth embedding (Lab 8). Target: class, yield, LAI, biomass, soil carbon. Few hundred to a few thousand labels — where tuned gradient-boosted trees have ruled.

Classical

For each dataset: fit \(\theta\), search hyper-parameters, cross-validate, ensemble. Hours of compute per table.

Tabular FM

Train once on millions of tables. A new table is the prompt: no gradient step, no tuning.

Inference: the whole labelled training set \(D_{\text{train}}=\{(\mathbf{x}_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\) is the context $$q_\theta\big(y_{\text{test}}\mid \mathbf{x}_{\text{test}},\,D_{\text{train}}\big)\quad\text{in one forward pass}$$ Pre-training: hide one label in a table drawn from \(p(D)\), predict it from the rest $$\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{D\sim p(D)}\,\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in D}\big[-\log q_\theta(y\mid \mathbf{x},D_{\text{train}})\big]$$ Minimiser = the Bayesian posterior predictive over data-generating mechanisms \(\phi\): $$q_\theta\;\to\;p(y\mid\mathbf{x},D_{\text{train}})=\!\int p(y\mid\mathbf{x},\phi)\,p(\phi\mid D_{\text{train}})\,d\phi$$

The forward pass is the inference algorithm: it averages over every hypothesis consistent with the context, without ever writing \(\phi\) down.

Optimal only relative to its prior \(p(D)\): data from a mechanism outside it give a model that is not unsure but confidently wrong.
This is the Prior-data Fitted Network (PFN) idea of Müller et al. (2022), turned into a practical tabular model by TabPFN (Hollmann et al. 2023; v2 in Nature 2025). The convergence result needs three conditions: enough capacity, enough training, and — the one usually forgotten — a prior whose support contains the real data-generating mechanism. The first two are engineering; the third is a modelling assumption that more compute cannot fix (Manero et al. 2026, §2.1). This is the same in-context learning that LLMs do with text (Part X), but over the rows and columns of a table. The next interactive slide computes this posterior predictive exactly for a toy prior so you can see what the network is trained to imitate.

Where the knowledge comes from: prior and architecture

① Synthetic prior

Sample a random structural causal model: a DAG, edge functions (small MLPs, tree rules, discretisations), noise. Features and target are nodes of the same graph: \(z_j=f_j(z_{\mathrm{pa}(j)},\varepsilon_j),\;\mathbf{x}=z_S,\;y=z_t\).
TabPFN, TabICL, TabFM, EXAONE Tabular — 10⁸ tables, none real, no leakage.

② Real tables

Pre-train on corpora of real tables; embed column names and cell text so “NDVI” or “soil_type” carry meaning: \(e_{ij}=\psi(\text{name}_j,\text{value}_{ij})\).
ConTextTab / SAP-RPT (semantic), TabDPT (values + retrieval).

③ Hybrid

Start from a synthetic prior, continue pre-training on curated real tables.
Real-TabPFN, Real-TabPFN-2.5. The synthetic-vs-real split is softening.

Alternating attention costs \(\mathcal{O}(d\,n^2+n\,d^2)\) per layer for \(n\) rows and \(d\) features — row compression and retrieval of a query-specific context (TabDPT, LoCalPFN) are how models escape it.

The prior is the organising variable of this literature (Manero et al. 2026). In the SCM prior the target is just another node, so it can be a parent of some features, not only a function of them; missing values, outliers and categorical columns must be manufactured deliberately in post-processing, otherwise the model never learns to handle them. Real-data models gain semantics (a column literally named 'Country') but lose control over contamination with benchmark data. Permutation: row order never carries meaning, so every architecture here treats the context as a set. Column-order equivariance holds only when the feature axis has no positional encoding (TabPFN v2); row-compression designs give it up in exchange for a row representation whose size no longer grows with the number of features.

What the network learns: the posterior predictive

true mechanism prior p(D)
Here the prior is a family of Gaussian processes, \(\phi=(\ell,\sigma)\): length-scale and noise. With \(K_\phi\) the kernel matrix of the context, $$p(\phi\mid D)\propto p(\phi)\,\mathcal{N}\!\big(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{0},K_\phi+\sigma^2 I\big)$$ and the prediction is the mixture \(\sum_\phi p(\phi\mid D)\,p(y\mid\mathbf{x},D,\phi)\)

A PFN learns this map

We can compute it exactly only because the prior is tiny. A tabular FM's prior has millions of mechanisms; the transformer amortises the integral into one forward pass.

Pick threshold + smooth only and add rows: the band stays narrow while missing the step, and coverage falls as rows are added (≈ 67 % → 52 %). That is prior misspecification.
Left: context points (terracotta; gold ones are points you clicked), the posterior-predictive mean (blue) and 90 % band, and the true mechanism (dashed green). Right: how the posterior weight is spread over length-scales — the model averages over hypotheses rather than picking one. With few rows the band is wide and the weight is spread out; as n grows the weight concentrates. The 'single best φ' in the readout is what per-dataset tuning gives (type-II maximum likelihood): with few rows it can under-cover (saturating, n = 8: 86 % against 99 % for the average) because it ignores the uncertainty about φ itself; with many rows the two coincide. Threshold mechanisms, such as a water/land boundary in NDWI, are exactly what a smooth-only prior (ℓ ≥ 0.4) cannot express: with 40 rows its 90 % band covers only about half of new points, and more data makes it worse, not better. The saturating curve, which the narrow prior can express, stays near 89 %. Switching to the broad prior repairs the threshold case (≈ 84 %). This is the argument for richer priors — tree-generated tables (TabForestPFN), mixtures of priors (Mitra), drift-aware priors (Helli et al. 2024). TabArena: who wins, where, and at what cost

TabArena BeyondArena
Elo from pairwise wins over datasets: $$P(A\succ B)=\frac{1}{1+10^{(R_B-R_A)/400}}$$ +400 Elo ≈ 10 : 1 (91 %). Anchor: a default random forest = 1000.

Scoring rules: RMSE only sees the mean

Continuous ranked probability score (lower is better) $$\mathrm{CRPS}(F,y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big(F(z)-\mathbb{1}\{z\ge y\}\big)^2dz$$ a point forecast gives \(|y-\hat y|\). Interval score for \([l,u]\) at level \(1-\alpha\): $$\mathrm{IS}_\alpha=(u-l)+\tfrac{2}{\alpha}(l-y)_+ +\tfrac{2}{\alpha}(y-u)_+$$

ScoringBench (2026)

101 regression datasets, 50+ models. Rankings change with the scoring rule; EXAONE Tabular and TabPFN-3 lead on CRPS. Wrapping TabICLv2 in conformal prediction did not improve its interval score.

For LAI, yield or biomass maps report CRPS or interval score and coverage, not only RMSE.
Three forecasters for the same heteroscedastic target (uncertainty grows from left to right, like biomass saturation). A has the true mean but intervals that are too narrow; B has a noisier mean but honest intervals; C ignores heteroscedasticity and is biased. RMSE and MAE rank A first because they see only the mean. CRPS, the interval score and the log score reward calibration and sharpness together, so B wins until A's spread approaches the true σ. Set C's bias to 0: its single spread is wrong everywhere but its mean is exact, and it can edge out B on CRPS while still losing on the interval and log scores — no single number tells the whole story. The log score punishes over-confidence hardest. PIT histograms: flat = calibrated, U-shape = too narrow, slope = bias. Tabular FMs output full predictive distributions (TabPFN: a histogram over bins; TabICLv2, EXAONE: many quantiles), so evaluate them with a proper scoring rule chosen for the decision at hand — e.g. a weighted CRPS on the right tail for flood depth (Landsgesell et al. 2026). Good fits

  • Field-plot regression: yield, biomass, soil properties — 10²–10⁴ samples
  • Classifying embeddings or pixel features from a few hundred labels
  • A fast, strong baseline before any tuning — and free uncertainty

Watch out

  • Spatial autocorrelation: always compare with GroupKFold blocks, not random folds
  • Wall-to-wall inference: ≈ 0.1–0.5 s per 1 000 rows on a GPU → hours for a 10⁸-pixel scene; distil or tile
  • Operating range (rows, features, ≤ 10 classes for some), licence, calibration

Calibration: does 90 % mean 90 %?

Temperature scaling of the logits \(\mathbf{z}\): $$\hat{\mathbf{p}}=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z}/T)$$ Expected calibration error over confidence bins \(B_m\): $$\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_m\frac{|B_m|}{n}\,\big|\operatorname{acc}(B_m)-\operatorname{conf}(B_m)\big|$$

Why EO needs it

Probabilities feed thresholds, area estimates, alerts and decisions. Deep networks trained with cross-entropy are usually over-confident; one scalar \(T\), fitted on held-out data, fixes much of it.

Left: reliability diagram. Blue bars are the accuracy in each confidence bin; the shaded part is the gap to the confidence the model claimed; the diagonal is perfect calibration. Right: how often each confidence occurs. The over-confident network puts most pixels near 100 % confidence but is right less often. Drag T above 1 (softer probabilities) or press “fit T”: ECE and NLL drop, accuracy does not change because the arg-max is the same. Calibration holds only for data like the validation set — after a domain shift it degrades again (two slides on).

Ensembles: aleatoric vs epistemic uncertainty

Each member \(m\) predicts a mean \(\mu_m\) and a noise variance \(\sigma_m^2\): $$\sigma^2(\mathbf{x})=\underbrace{\tfrac1M\textstyle\sum_m\sigma_m^2}_{\text{aleatoric}}+\underbrace{\tfrac1M\textstyle\sum_m(\mu_m-\bar\mu)^2}_{\text{epistemic}}$$

Two kinds of “don't know”

Aleatoric: noise in the data (saturation, speckle) — more data won't remove it. Epistemic: the model has not seen such inputs — it grows in gaps and outside the training range.

Conformal prediction wraps any model: widen intervals by the \((1-\alpha)\) quantile of held-out residuals to guarantee coverage.
Biomass from L-band backscatter saturates and gets noisier at high biomass (heteroscedastic, aleatoric noise). Each ensemble member is trained on a bootstrap sample with different random features; where they agree the dark (epistemic) band is thin. Inside the gap and beyond the data (gold zone) they disagree — that disagreement is the warning. With M = 1 there is no epistemic estimate at all. MC dropout approximates the same idea with one network. Global canopy-height and biomass maps (e.g. Lang et al. 2023) now ship a per-pixel uncertainty layer built this way.

Geographic bias: who is the model good for?

where the 600 training labels come from

The pattern

Most EO benchmarks and pre-training data come from Europe, North America and China. Accuracy is then lowest where maps are needed most — smallholder farms, drylands, informal settlements.

Report the worst region, not only the mean: $$\text{Acc}_{\min}=\min_{g}\ \text{Acc}_g$$ and train for it (group re-weighting, distributionally robust optimisation).
Kerner et al. (2024) found low agreement between existing cropland maps across Sub-Saharan Africa.
Six regions share the same three land-cover classes but differ in spectra: drier vegetation and brighter soils in North and Sub-Saharan Africa, haze and different crops elsewhere. With a typical benchmark mix (no African labels) the model is excellent in Europe and poor in Africa, although the mean accuracy looks acceptable. Move the slider: a modest share of local labels closes most of the gap at almost no cost elsewhere. The general lesson for every map you publish: report accuracy per region (or per city, per agro-ecological zone) and say where the model should not be used.

The carbon cost of training and inference

$$E=n_{\text{dev}}\cdot P\cdot t\cdot \text{PUE},\qquad \text{CO}_2\text{e}=E\cdot \text{CI}_{\text{grid}}$$
PUE: data-centre overhead · CI: carbon intensity of the electricity

Lighter options

  • Use embeddings or a frozen FM before full fine-tuning
  • Smaller models, mixed precision, early stopping
  • Run where and when the grid is clean
  • Report energy and CO₂e in the paper
Presets: fine-tuning a U-Net is a few kWh — negligible. Pre-training a foundation model on 64 A100s for two weeks is in the tonnes of CO₂e, comparable to several long-haul flights, and strongly dependent on the grid. Serving a model for months can exceed training. Numbers are orders of magnitude: real power draw is below the rated TDP, and embodied emissions from manufacturing the hardware are not counted. Zhu et al. (2026) list “carbon-minimised” as one of the 11 features of an ideal Earth FM.

Privacy, dual use and documentation

◉

Privacy

At 30–50 cm, houses, cars and gatherings are visible. Combined with other data, a map can identify people. Aggregate before publishing.

⚠

Dual use

The same detector counts ships for fisheries control or for military targeting; a settlement map guides upgrading — or evictions.

⇄

Communities

Involve the people being mapped: co-design, consent for field data, CARE principles for Indigenous data.

≡

Document

Datasheet for the labels, model card for the model (data, regions, limits, metrics per group), licence, FAIR release.

Before publishing a map, ask: who could be harmed by it, and who was not asked? Write the answer in the model card.
These four cards turn the lessons of the course into general rules. A model card is a one-page document: intended use, training data and regions, evaluation per group and region, known failure cases, ethical considerations. A datasheet does the same for a dataset: who collected the labels, when, how, with what consent and licence. Both take an afternoon and make a paper easier to review and reuse.
